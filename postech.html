Technical Walkthrough

At POSTECH's Bio-Inspired Robotics & Design Lab, I am developing a model-based control and simulation framework for a bio-inspired quadruped intended for challenging terrain and, ultimately, planetary exploration. The central idea is to use the same optimization-based control architecture for ordinary walking, vertical climbing, and transitions between surfaces, while investigating a compliant tail for stabilization and manipulation.

The current platform is a torque-actuated Unitree Go1 model simulated in MuJoCo. Walking, climbing, mixed contacts, and tail manipulation have been tested in simulation; the custom robot and physical climbing hardware remain development goals.

1. Project Overview

Most quadruped locomotion controllers assume contact with a horizontal floor. Climbing introduces different contact-force directions, while a ground-to-wall transition requires the robot to support its weight with feet attached to different surfaces at the same time. The research investigates how much of a single controller can be retained across these modes.

The project currently comprises four connected tracks: quadruped crawling, wall locomotion with prescribed contacts, a continuous floor-to-wall maneuver, and a four-tendon continuum tail. A separate parametric CAD workspace is being developed to translate the simulated geometry into manufacturable parts.

2. Model-Based Locomotion Architecture

I implemented a hierarchical whole-body controller in which every layer solves a specific problem. The gait scheduler chooses when a foot moves; the foothold planner chooses where it should land; MPC plans the supporting contact forces; a velocity-level QP translates body and foot motion into joint commands; and a joint controller produces actuator torques.

The baseline uses a slow, statically supported crawl with one swinging foot at a time. A Raibert-style foothold rule accounts for desired and measured body velocity. During swing, the foot follows a smooth fifth-order trajectory with a vertical clearance bump.

\[p_d(s)=p_0+(10s^3-15s^4+6s^5)(p_1-p_0)+\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\H\,64s^3(1-s)^3\end{bmatrix}\]

Here, \(s\in[0,1]\) is the normalized swing phase, \(p_0\) and \(p_1\) are the liftoff and touchdown positions, and \(H=0.045\,\mathrm{m}\). The resulting desired foot velocity becomes a task in the whole-body QP.

3. Model Predictive Control

The core of the system is a convex receding-horizon MPC that predicts the robot's center-of-mass motion and selects the ground-reaction forces required to track the desired body trajectory. A simplified single-rigid-body model keeps the optimization fast enough for repeated online solving.

State and Dynamics

The 12-dimensional robot state and 12-dimensional input are:

\[x=\begin{bmatrix}p^T&v^T&\eta^T&\omega^T\end{bmatrix}^{T},\qquad u=\begin{bmatrix}f_{FL}^T&f_{FR}^T&f_{RL}^T&f_{RR}^T\end{bmatrix}^{T}\]

Position \(p\), velocity \(v\), orientation \(\eta\), and angular velocity \(\omega\) define the predicted body motion. The four foot forces \(f_i\) influence both the linear and angular acceleration:

\[m\ddot p=\sum_{i=1}^{4}f_i+mg,\qquad I_W\dot\omega=\sum_{i=1}^{4}(r_i-p)\times f_i\]

After discretization, the system has affine dynamics \(x_{k+1}=Ax_k+B_ku_k+d\), allowing the controller to solve a quadratic program. It uses 15 prediction steps of 40 ms, providing a 0.60 s look-ahead horizon.

Optimization Objective

The controller balances accurate body-state tracking against the magnitude and rate of change of support forces:

\[\min_{x,u}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\!\left[\|x_{k+1}-x^{ref}_{k+1}\|^2_Q+\|u_k\|^2_R+\|u_k-u_{k-1}\|^2_S\right]\]

Contact constraints require stance feet to push within a linearized friction cone, while a swinging foot must apply zero force. The scheduled contact sequence is supplied externally; MPC optimizes forces, not gait timing or foothold locations. I use OSQP to solve the convex QP and apply only the first optimized force action before replanning.

4. Whole-Body IK-QP and Torque Control

The MPC determines how the body should move and how support forces should be distributed, but it does not directly compute the twelve motor commands. For this, I implemented a separate whole-body velocity inverse-kinematics QP that works at a higher update rate.

Instantaneous Velocity Optimization

The optimization variable is the 18-dimensional generalized velocity \(\nu\): six floating-base velocities and twelve joint velocities. MuJoCo provides the COM, body rotation, and foot Jacobians. The QP follows the desired COM and body orientation, tracks the swinging foot, and regularizes joint posture while imposing approximately stationary stance feet.

\[\min_{\nu}\;w_b^2\|J_C\nu-v_b^*\|^2+w_a^2\|J_R\nu-\omega_b^*\|^2+\sum_{i\in\mathcal S}w_f^2\|J_i\nu-v_i^*\|^2+\cdots\]

Joint-position and velocity limits are enforced in the QP. The optimized joint velocities are integrated into position targets, then tracked by an explicit joint PD controller. Planned MPC contact forces enter the motors through a separate Jacobian-transpose feedforward term.

\[\tau=\tau_{PD}+\tau_{ff}\]

The simulation advances at 500 Hz, whole-body IK-QP updates at 250 Hz, and the centroidal MPC runs at 25 Hz. This separation allows slower force planning and faster joint-level motion tracking.

5. Vertical Climbing with the Same Controller

The next step was to extend the walking architecture to a vertical wall. Rather than introducing an entirely different controller, I retained the MPC, whole-body QP, and PD structure and changed the surface geometry, contact-force constraints, and body-orientation reference.

Surface-Aligned Contact Forces

For each contacting foot, a surface frame defines a forward direction \(t_f\), a lateral direction \(t_l\), and a normal \(n\). The optimized forces still live in world coordinates; the surface frame only transforms them for contact constraints.

\[R_{s,i}=\begin{bmatrix}t_{f,i}&t_{l,i}&n_i\end{bmatrix},\qquad f_i^s=R_{s,i}^T f_i^W\]

A ground contact can push on the floor but not pull it. An attached wall foot, by contrast, must also be able to supply tensile forces against gravity. The simulation models wall attachment with ideal MuJoCo constraints; real microspine or adhesive gripping is not yet validated.

Feline-Inspired Climbing Posture

I also explored an alternative climbing posture inspired by feline movement: the trunk is referenced closer to the wall, the front feet are placed higher, and the rear feet lower. The wall orientation is tracked through a local rotation-vector error instead of an absolute Euler-angle representation, avoiding the singularity near the head-up wall pose.

6. Walking-to-Wall Transition

Walking and climbing separately are not enough: the difficult part is the transition in which rear feet remain on the ground while front feet attach to the wall. This is a mixed-contact optimization problem, because each foot may have a different surface orientation and force model at the same instant.

I extended the contact representation from a single global surface to per-foot, per-horizon-step modes:

\[m_{i,k}\in\{\mathrm{GROUND},\;\mathrm{WALL},\;\mathrm{SWING}\}\]

A foot in ground contact receives unilateral friction constraints; a wall-attached foot receives an idealized attachment force box; and a swinging foot receives zero contact force. The centroidal dynamics, MPC objective, and solver remain unchanged. The transition itself uses a prescribed sequence of approach, rearing, front attachment, incremental climbing steps, rear transfer, and final wall locomotion.

A stored 47-second MuJoCo run completes the transition, attaches all four feet to the wall, enters feline-style climbing, and records no MPC or IK-QP failures. The result is an important proof of the mixed-contact software architecture, but does not yet demonstrate autonomous contact selection or physical attachment mechanics.

7. Tendon-Driven Soft-Tail Control

In parallel with locomotion, I am investigating a compliant tail as a potential tool for stabilization, exploration, and manipulation. The simulated experimental tail is 43 cm long, uses four antagonistic tendons, and contains three regions of progressively decreasing bending stiffness. The tail is modeled as an elastic cable in MuJoCo rather than as a chain of rigid actuated joints.

Three-Section PCC and Gravity-Aware Modeling

I model the continuum geometry using Piecewise Constant Curvature (PCC), representing each section with two bending coordinates. Four tendon lengths form two aggregate antagonistic bending commands; the section distribution is obtained using calibrated gains or an experimental gravity-aware equilibrium model. The sections are not independently actuated.

\[q=\begin{bmatrix}b_{y,1}&b_{z,1}&b_{y,2}&b_{z,2}&b_{y,3}&b_{z,3}\end{bmatrix}^{T}\]

The gravity-aware model predicts a plausible elastic equilibrium from tendon constraints, stiffness, and gravity; PCC forward kinematics then estimate the tail-tip position. A tendon-to-tip Jacobian describes how changing tendon lengths moves the tip:

\[\hat p_{tip}=f_{PCC}(q^*(l)),\qquad J=\frac{\partial \hat p_{tip}}{\partial l}=\frac{\partial \hat p_{tip}}{\partial q^*}\frac{\partial q^*}{\partial l}\]

Tendon QP and Force Control

A convex tendon-rate QP selects four tendon motions that best realize a target tip velocity while respecting travel limits, tendon speed limits, smoothing, and neutral-length regularization. The desired tendon lengths are integrated from those rates, then tracked with low-level PID tension control.

\[\dot l^*=\arg\min_{\dot l}\;w_t\|J\dot l-v_{target}\|^2+w_m\|\dot l\|^2+w_s\|\dot l-\dot l_{prev}\|^2+\cdots\]

Separate experiments also investigate coupling tail curvature to the centroidal MPC through an inertial-reaction model. In a matched disturbance test with the earlier one-section tail, the tail-aware controller reduced pitch RMS by 8.16% over the disturbance-and-recovery window. The improvement was not uniform across other metrics, so this remains exploratory.

Object Wrapping and Pickup

The graded tail has also completed an isolated simulated grasp: it wraps laterally around a small cylinder, then bends in an orthogonal plane to lift it. The recorded experiment sustained a 23.45 mm cylinder-center lift and retained the free object in the air for 4 seconds, without an artificial weld. The scene uses a fixed object pose and staging collar; it is not a perception-driven autonomous grasp.

The next challenges are replacing ideal wall attachments with realistic gripping mechanics, estimating contact geometry from perception, planning contact transitions autonomously, and transferring the algorithms to a custom physical robot.
